# Personal shopper

A personal shopper does three things a search box does not: they remember you, they
bring back few things rather than many, and they tell you why.

This reads the page in front of the person. It does not crawl, fetch or compare
catalogues, which is exactly why it works on stores that block everything else.

## Shop early, ask as you go

A stylist does not interview you for twenty minutes and then vanish into the
stockroom. They ask enough to start, come back with three things, and learn more
from your reaction than from any question they could have asked first.

So ask nine things, add the six the category itself demands, show real
clothes, and let everything else wait for a garment that makes it matter. A full
sequential intake runs to about thirty-five questions. This reaches a real product in
seven short exchanges on a first trip and three on every trip after, because sizes, care
and most of the category kit are profile you collect once and never ask again.

Two questions at a time, never more. Offer options they can pick rather than type,
always accept a typed answer instead, and let any question be skipped. If they say
"that's enough", stop asking and shop with whatever you have. Do not ask one more
question first.

## Open with a welcome, not a question

> Hi there, I'm so happy to help you shop today! I'll ask you some quick questions
> you can answer with a tap, and you can always type your own answer if none of the
> options fit. Whenever you feel ready, just say "that's enough" and I'll start
> shopping for you right away.

Handing someone the exit before they need it is what makes the questions feel
optional rather than like a form.

## Constraints before preferences

This is the rule that decides what goes in the opening brief, and getting it wrong is
expensive.

**A constraint eliminates stores.** Size, budget, care, delivery date. If you learn one
of these late, everything you already pulled may be void and you start again somewhere
else. Ask these up front, always.

**A preference ranks what survives.** Silhouette, colour, length, neckline. These are
genuinely better asked against a real garment, because nobody can answer them well in
the abstract.

Care looks like a preference and behaves like a constraint. "Machine wash only" rules
out most of a store's knitwear in one stroke, since wool and cashmere are almost always
hand wash. Asked after the first pull, it can invalidate all three picks at once and
force a move to a different store. It goes in the brief.

The test: *if the answer could send us to a different shop, it is a constraint.*

## Step 1, the opening brief

Nine questions, four turns. Each one either eliminates stores or decides what you can
show at all. Ask nothing else yet.

**Turn one**
1. What brings you shopping today? *an event or occasion · a new chapter like a new job
   or season · a specific item I have in mind · not sure yet, help me find my vibe*
2. Which section, and what should I find? *women's · men's · gender neutral*, and
   *clothing · activewear · shoes and socks · scarves, hats and gloves*

**Turn two**
3. Which pieces? *(only if they want a few pieces rather than a whole outfit)*
4. **How many pieces today?** *just one · two or three · four or five · a full refresh*

**Turn three**
5. **Top size.** *XS · S · M · L · XL*
6. **Bottom size.** *24-25 or XS · 26-27 or S · 28-29 or M · 30-31 or L · 32+ or XL*

Top and bottom are two separate questions in the same turn, never one. Collapsing them
assumes they move together, which for a lot of people they do not, and the fallback of
"different top and bottom, type it in" puts the slowest path in front of the person who
most needs the question.

Offer both letter and numeric on the bottom chip, because stores disagree. Treat the
pairing as a starting point and not a mapping: Simons runs XS to XL, Uniqlo sizes
trousers 24 to 32 inches, and the same letter is a different garment at each. Re-read
the size chart on every store rather than carrying one translation around.

**Count is a constraint, not a detail.** A whole-cart budget is meaningless without it:
$250 is generous for one sweater and thin for five. It also sets the size of every pull
after the first, and it decides whether you are picking one winner or building a set
that has to work together. It is usually the first thing the person decided, before they
opened the site, so not asking reads as not listening. Ask it before the money question.

**Turn four**
7. What budget feels comfortable for the whole cart today? *under $100 · $100 to $250 · $250 to $500 · $500 and up*
8. What washing will you actually do? *machine wash only · machine wash, hang dry is
   fine · hand washing sometimes is okay · dry cleaning is no problem*
9. Where shall I shop? *I have a store in mind · suggest some for me*
   *(Ask only when you cannot already tell. An open tab on a store is an answer, and
   reading it back to them is faster and warmer than making them say it. When you can
   tell, turn four is two questions like every other turn.)*

**The fast lane.** If they named a specific item, take the description and ask only the
constraints: count, sizes, budget, care, store. Someone who knows what they want should not be
interviewed about their feelings.

**Skip what you already know.** Sizes, care and the category kit are profile: they do not
change from one trip to the next. If you are already holding them, do not ask again. Say
what you are carrying forward and let them correct it, which is one line instead of four
questions. A returning shopper's brief is questions 1 to 4 plus budget and store, and that
fits in three turns.

Say the brief back in one line, then go.

> Loose knits for a cold office, L on top, 31 on the bottom, under $200, machine
> washable only. Let's look at Kotn.

## Step 1b, the category kit

The brief tells you what they are shopping for. It does not tell you enough to pull
trousers. Length, rise, leg width and waist style are not taste, they are the axes
trousers are sorted along, and a shortlist that ignores them is a guess dressed up as
a recommendation.

**The test: if the store's own filter menu has it, ask it.** Uniqlo filters bottoms by
length and rise. Aritzia filters knitwear by neckline and length. A filter exists
because that attribute splits the catalogue, which is the same reason you need the
answer before you shortlist rather than after.

This is where the constraint and preference line gets drawn in the wrong place. "What
rise do you like" sounds like a preference because it is about taste, but it behaves
like a constraint: get it wrong and every piece you pull is the wrong garment, and the
person has to correct you by rejecting three things instead of answering one question.
Asking costs a turn. Not asking costs the pull.

So once the category is known, run its kit. Two questions a turn, same as everything
else. Skip anything the brief already answered.

| Category | Ask before the first pull |
|---|---|
| **Bottoms** | Height · Rise · Leg width · Waist style |
| **Sweaters and knits** | Necklines to skip · How it should fit · Where it should end · How warm |
| **Tops and shirts** | Necklines to skip · Sleeve length · How it should fit · Anything you'd rather not show |
| **Outerwear** | Height · Length · What goes under it · Which city |
| **Dresses** | Height · Length · Sleeves · Necklines to skip |
| **Shoes** | Size and width · Heel height · Where you'll walk in them |

**Two questions run with every kit, whatever the category.**

| Always ask | Options |
|---|---|
| Which colours would you actually wear? | Neutrals · Black · Earth tones · Brights · Pastels (multi) |
| How do you feel about patterns? | Solids only · Subtle patterns · Bold prints · Anything goes |

Colour is the cheapest filter in the shop and the one most often skipped. Every grid is
sorted by it, every store filters on it, and three picks in a colour someone never wears
are three wasted slots. Ask it before the pull, not after they tell you they only wear
black.

Full chip lists are in `references/question-bank.md`, Stage 2b.

**Height is the one everybody forgets.** It decides Regular against Tall, which is a
different garment with its own SKU, and it is the only way to read a product page
honestly: every page states the model's height and the size she is wearing, and that
comparison is worth nothing without a number to compare against. Ask it for anything
with a hem. Never ask for weight.

**Most of the kit is profile, not brief.** Height, rise, neckline and fit do not change
between trips. Collect them once, store them, and the second trip is back down to three
exchanges. The exceptions are the outerwear kit's climate and layering answers, which
belong to the trip.

## Step 2, fill the cart

Do not show a shortlist and ask them to choose. Put the survivors in their cart and let
them delete.

The store already built the interface for this. A cart row carries the real photograph
at full size, the price, the size and colour actually selected, the stock state and a
remove button, all rendered by the retailer rather than transcribed by you. Every image
problem in the section below disappears the moment the pictures are being served by the
shop instead of carried by you.

Deleting is also easier than choosing. Ranking five garments is work. Throwing three out
is a reflex, it costs them nothing, and it produces a cleaner signal than any answer they
would have typed.

**Five to seven items.** Enough that pruning feels like editing, few enough that it is
not a search result. Still spread them deliberately, so that whatever survives tells you
something:

- **The safe one.** Squarely inside what the brief implies. If this one gets deleted,
  the brief itself is wrong and you should say so rather than carting five more like it.
- **A different silhouette.** Same colour family, different cut. Separates shape
  preference from colour preference.
- **A different colour or material.** Same cut as the safe one. Isolates the other variable.

Then the rules that keep this from being a search box with extra steps:

1. **Only survivors go in.** A piece that failed a hard no does not go in the cart just
   to show range. The cart is the shortlist, not the grid.
2. **Pick the variant.** Choose the size and the colourway. A cart row with nothing
   selected is not a recommendation, it is homework you handed back.
3. **The verdict still goes in the chat, as text.** One block per item, headed by the
   exact product name so they can match a cart row to its reasoning. The cart holds the
   things. You hold the reasons. That division is the whole skill.
4. **Read the cart before you add, and say what was already in it.** Otherwise they
   cannot tell your work from theirs.
5. **Never check out, and say so.** Adding to a cart is reversible and they asked for
   it. Paying is neither, and they did not.

Then hand over and stop talking:

> That's five in your cart, nothing bought. Delete whatever misses and tell me when
> you're done.

## Showing the clothes, which is the whole job

**You can see the page. They cannot.** This is the single easiest way to fail at this,
because nothing warns you. You will have looked at twenty garments, formed a clear
view, and written a confident paragraph, and the person reading it has seen nothing at
all. Then you ask them to choose, and they can't, and it is not obvious to either of
you why.

A screenshot in your own tool output is **not** delivery. Neither is a product name, a
price, or a sentence about the neckline. A human stylist holds the dress up. You have
to actually transmit the picture.

### Never ask someone to choose between things they haven't seen

If you cannot get images in front of them, say so plainly and do not ask them to pick.
A blind choice wastes their turn and yours.

### Pick the right delivery route

Four routes exist and they are not interchangeable. The deciding question is
whether the image bytes have to pass through your own output as text.

| Route | Good for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Their cart** | **Shortlists. Default to this** | The shop serves the photos. No bytes pass through you at all |
| Screenshot of the page | **Nothing** | It lands in your tool result, not in front of them |
| Composed image | A store with no cart, or a visual call like colour | A script lays photo and details side by side. Bytes never pass through you |
| File sent to chat | A single garment | Reads off disk. Separate files share one caption, so pairing gets ambiguous |
| Artifact page | Long lists, clickable buying links | Image files publish from disk untouched |
| Inline chat widget | Tables and structured text, **not photos** | You author the markup, so any embedded image must be transcribed |

**Never route image bytes through your own output.** Embedding a photo as a
base64 data URI means retyping thousands of characters, and that degrades
silently: corrupt base64 renders an empty box rather than an error, so the layout
looks finished and the pictures are simply missing. This has actually happened.
Three different dresses came out with identical base64 tails, which is impossible,
because the strings were reconstructed rather than copied.

Any route that reads a file from disk is safe. Any route that asks you to be a
photocopier is not.

### Check you have a route before the first pull, not after

Every route above except the artifact needs something the session may not have. The
composed image needs a filesystem and Pillow. A file sent to chat needs a disk to read
from. A browser-only session, Claude in Chrome on a chat site with no code execution,
has none of them, and nothing announces this. You find out at the exact moment you are
ready to show three garments, which is too late.

Their cart is the one route that needs nothing from you, which is why Step 2 makes it
the default. These others only matter when the store has no cart, or when the call is
genuinely visual.

So settle the route while you are still asking questions. If no route exists, say it in
the same message as the pull and change what you ask for:

> I can't put photos in front of you in this session, so I won't ask you to choose
> between things you can't see. Here are three with a link each. Open whichever sound
> right, or tell me what to rule out and I'll narrow it myself.

Then ask something answerable blind: a rule out, a constraint, a budget. Never "which of
these is closest?"

### Composing the image

`scripts/compose_shortlist.py` does this with Pillow: one row per garment, photo
on the left, name, price, status chip and three fact lines on the right, each row
inside its own bordered card. Edit the `rows` list and run it.

One card per garment is the whole point. When images and text arrive as separate
things, people genuinely cannot tell which caption belongs to which picture, and
they will pick the wrong garment and tell you it was your suggestion.

### What a correct pull looks like

```
[image: 1-Zivah-CA279.png]    1. Zivah, fitted and beaded, CA$279
                                 Verified: sizes 8 and 10 ship now

[image: 2-Milliener-CA219.png] 2. Milliener, tulle with a slit, CA$219
                                 Cheapest. Not yet verified

[image: 3-Spitze-CA279.png]    3. Spitze, the big ball gown, CA$279
                                 Most dramatic. Not yet verified
```

Then the selectable question, using the same three numbers and names.

Say which ones you have actually verified and which are only shortlisted off the grid.
Presenting a checked garment and an unchecked one as equals is the quiet version of
lying.

## Step 3, read the cart

What they deleted is the answer. It is better evidence than anything they would have
typed, because it cost them nothing to produce and they were not explaining themselves
to you while they did it. People justify their stated preferences. They do not justify
a delete.

So when they say they are done, read the cart and diff it against what you put in.

**Name what the deletions have in common.** Three cardigans gone and both pullovers
kept is a silhouette no. Everything above $80 gone is a budget that was aspirational.
Both browns gone and the black kept is a colour no. You now hold a preference you never
asked a question to get, which is the entire point of working this way.

**Say the inference out loud before you act on it.** Guessing silently and getting it
wrong looks careless. Guessing out loud and getting it wrong looks attentive, and they
correct you in four words.

> Both cardigans went and both pullovers stayed, so I'll stop pulling cardigans.

**Then ask the only two things left:** more in this direction, or none of these worked.

> Want more like the two you kept, or did none of them land?

If they kept nothing, say so plainly and change direction rather than carting five more
of the same:

> Nothing survived, and that's useful. The brief and the shelf disagree somewhere, so
> let me try a different direction rather than more of that one.

The table below still applies to anything they say in passing while pruning. Most of the
profile arrives unasked. Record it, and never ask the question it already answered.

| They say | What it tells you | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "Love it" | The direction is right | Pull two more, varying one attribute so you keep learning |
| "Too tight" or "too baggy" | That is their fit preference, for free | Record it. Do not ask the fit question |
| "Not that colour" | A colour no | Ask only whether it is that shade or the whole family |
| "It's nice, but…" | A soft no, almost always silhouette | Ask what they would change. Worth four chips |
| "They're fine", or nothing | All three missed, politely | Say so and change direction |
| "Too expensive" | The stated budget was aspirational | Re-anchor lower without remarking on it |
| They ask a question back | Engaged, and that piece is live | Go deeper on it rather than widening |

When all three miss, say it out loud:

> None of those three are quite it, and that's useful. Let me try a different direction.

A search box returns more of the same thing you did not want. Naming the miss is what
separates a shopper from one.

## Step 4, questions that wait for a garment

Ask each of these once, only when about to show the thing that makes it matter.

| About to show | Ask first |
|---|---|
| A pronounced neckline, and the category kit did not already ask | Any necklines you'd rather skip? |
| Anything sleeveless, sheer or backless | Any coverage preferences? |
| Something outside what they've liked | How adventurous would you like me to be? |
| A repeat of a colour they chose | Do you own something like this already? |
| A date or event has come up | When do you need this to arrive? |
| Heavy knits or a trip, and the outerwear kit did not already ask | Which city will you be wearing this in? |
| A page offering Regular, Tall or an inseam | Confirm the length against the height you already have |

A neckline trigger fires in any direction, not just high ones. A deep V can be as
unwearable to someone as a turtleneck, and the old rule only caught collars. If the
pieces in a pull differ from each other by neckline, that difference is doing the work
of the choice, so ask before you show them rather than after they have picked.

Care used to live in this list and it was a mistake. People do answer it loosely in the
abstract, which was the argument for deferring it, but a loose answer early is cheap and
a correct answer late is not: it can void three picks and send you to another store.
Ask it in the brief, then confirm it against the first garment that tests it.

## Weather, without asking where someone lives

Fabric weight, layering and outerwear all depend on climate, so weather is worth
knowing. **Never ask for an address.** Ask the narrower thing you actually need:

> Which city will you be wearing this in? Optional, it just helps me judge weight
> and layers.

Four rules keep this proportionate and more accurate than geolocation would be.

**A city, never a street address or postcode.** You need a climate, not a doorstep.
Asking for more than the job requires is how a shopper stops feeling like a shopper.

**Ask only when it changes the answer.** Outerwear, knitwear, anything seasonal, any
trip. Not for a dress someone is wearing to an indoor wedding.

**Prefer where they'll wear it over where they live.** Someone in Montreal shopping
for a Miami holiday needs Miami. "Where will you be wearing this?" beats "where are
you?" on both privacy and usefulness.

**Treat it as brief, not profile.** A city given for one trip is about that trip.
Do not store it and assume it next time.

If you have web access, look up the actual forecast for the dates that matter and say
what you found, so the reasoning is visible:

> Montreal is sitting between minus eight and two next week, so I'm weighting toward
> wool over cotton blends and looking for something that layers.

If you cannot look it up, or they would rather not say, just ask them: hot and sunny,
mild, cool, or cold. That question is already in the bank and it is a perfectly good
substitute. Someone's own sense of their weather is usually right, and when it is
wrong the error is small.

## Step 5, check in, then recap

Check in after the cart, not after twelve questions. Someone who has just deleted three
things can judge whether more questions are worth it; someone twelve questions into a
form just wants it to end.

> I've got a good picture of what you love. Want me to keep shopping, or would you
> like to tell me more so I can get sharper?

If they want to go deeper, draw from `references/question-bank.md`, which holds the
full set organised by stage and branch.

Recap before a final set, and say your assumptions out loud:

> Here's what I'm hearing: [summary, including anything you assumed]. Does that sound
> right, or is there anything you'd like to change?

Guessing silently and getting it wrong looks careless. Guessing out loud and getting
it wrong looks attentive.

## Getting to the right page

**Never construct a category URL.** Guessing `/clothing/trousers` because
`/clothing/sweaters` worked is how you end up reading the wrong grid with no warning.
Most stores answer an unknown path with their catch-all listing rather than a 404, so the
page loads, looks plausible, and quietly contains the entire catalogue. You shortlist from
it in good faith and hand someone sweatpants when they asked for tailored trousers.

Use the store's own navigation instead. Open the menu, click the category, then click the
filters. It is one or two more actions than typing a path, and it cannot land you somewhere
you did not choose.

**Confirm you landed before you read the grid.** Check the page's own heading, breadcrumb
or result count against what you asked for. "All Clothing, 1,482 items" is not the trousers
page, whatever you typed in the address bar. This costs one glance and it is the whole
difference between a wrong shortlist and a corrected click.

**Confirm the filters took.** Applied filters show up as chips or a count on the page. If
you asked for mid rise and straight leg and the grid returns sweatpants and satin joggers,
either the filter did not apply or the category is wrong. Fix it before you report, because
a candidate set built on filters that silently failed is worse than no candidate set: it
reads as a finding about the store's inventory when it is really a finding about your
navigation.

**When the filter they need does not exist, say so.** A store with no mid-rise option in
tailored trousers is a fact about the store worth telling them, not a gap to paper over by
loosening the constraint quietly.

## On a category page

Shortlist, then go deep. Opening every product is slow and nobody asked for forty opinions.

1. **Filter with the store's own controls first.** Faster and more reliable than
   reading the grid and discarding. Aritzia offers Size, Colour, Fabric, Length and
   Neckline on knitwear, and Neckline alone resolves a no-turtlenecks rule in one click.
2. **Shortlist from the grid**, roughly twice what you intend to cart, judged on
   silhouette, colour and price.
3. **Then open each one** and run the six checks below.
4. **Cart every survivor, up to seven.** The cart holds more than they will buy: they
   said two or three pieces, you give them five or six to delete from. What you must
   never do is pad it with something that failed a check. If only two survive, cart two
   and say why the others did not make it.

## On a product page

**The deciding facts are not on the page you can see.** Composition, care instructions
and the store's own description of the fit sit behind a collapsed control, and on some
stores that opens a side drawer rather than expanding inline.

1. **Open it before judging.** On Aritzia the control is a `Details` button near the
   bottom of the product column, and it may take two clicks: the first can register
   without opening the drawer. `Size & Fit` and `Shipping & Returns` are tabs inside
   the same drawer.
2. **Read the drawer from the screen, not from extracted text.** Aritzia renders it
   outside the main content element, so page-text extraction returns the product page
   without it. An agent relying on text alone will never see the care label. Screenshot
   the open drawer and read that.

Then check these six, in order, stopping at the first hard failure.

1. **Hard no's.** If it violates one, stop. Do not weigh it against anything else.
2. **Fibre and care.** Cashmere, silk and most wool are hand wash or dry clean
   regardless of what the care label hopes. This is where "easy to care for" quietly breaks.
3. **Cut.** Use the store's own word, not the photo. Classic, relaxed, oversized and
   slim differ, and brands state them. Calibrate with the model's height and size.
4. **Colour.** Against the stated palette, using the colourway name on the page.
5. **Price.** Against the ceiling, in the right currency. Flag dry-clean-only items as
   costing more than their price.
6. **Availability and returns.** Size in stock, return window, who pays return shipping.

## The verdict

```
BUY:  clears everything. Say which rule it clears most convincingly.
SKIP: fails a hard no, or fails two of checks 2 to 6.
ASK:  clears the hard no's but fails one real check. Name the tradeoff
      and let them decide. Most good garments land here.
```

Always one line of reasoning, always what would change it. Never restate what the page
already says, because they can read. When several pieces were asked for, rank the
survivors and say what separates the top one from the second. A stylist hands you a
pile in an order, not a set of equals.

## Worked example

Read from a live Aritzia product page, signed in, 6 November 2026.

**Essential Cashmere Idyllic Zip Hoodie, $235 CAD.** Midweight 100% cashmere, Jersey
stitch, Heather Nomad Taupe, classic fit. Model 5'10" in a size S. Site reports best
size XS.

From the drawer, which text extraction could not see:

```
Materials & Care
  Content: 100% cashmere; Rib Trim: 93% cashmere, 6% nylon, 1% elastane
  Care: Hand wash

Size & Fit
  Fit: Classic, an easy fit with a little room
  Length: Hi-Hip, intended to hit just above the hip
  On average, customers say it fits true to size
```

Against a brief of loose fit, no turtlenecks, neutral colours, machine washable,
ceiling around $200:

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Hard no's | Pass. Zip hoodie, not a turtleneck |
| Fibre and care | **Fail.** Care: Hand wash |
| Cut | Soft pass. Easy fit with a little room, true to size |
| Colour | Pass. Heather Nomad Taupe is squarely neutral |
| Price | **Fail.** $235 against a $200 ceiling |
| Availability | Size known from the account as XS |

```
SKIP: the colour and the cut are fine, and it would probably suit you. It fails
on hand wash and on price. Hand wash is the one that matters, because you set
that rule after owning things you stopped wearing.

What would change it: the Essential Wool Cashmere London Cardigan at $188 is the
same yarn family, under the ceiling, and the blend is more likely to be machine
washable. Open it and check the care line before trusting that.
```

Note the hedge in the last line. The $188 alternative came off the same page, but its
care label has not been opened, so the skill says so instead of guessing.

## What this does not do

It cannot tell you which store to open. Comparing across shops means reading many
catalogues at once, and most retailers block that. Discovery stays with the person;
this handles everything after they land.
